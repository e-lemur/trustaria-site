From attested to demonstrable: a common model for continuous control evidence in digital assets

A vendor-neutral draft giving participants a shared vocabulary, two worked examples, and a set of open questions – not a product pitch.

1Purpose of this note

This draft gives an industry discussion a common vocabulary and a concrete, testable model. It sets out (i) the distinction between status and demonstrable evidence; (ii) the minimum records needed to make a control reconstructable; (iii) a mapping of representative MiCA and DORA obligations to that model; (iv) two worked examples; and (v) explicit unresolved questions for the next discussion.

It is written to be challenged and improved by issuers, custodians, compliance specialists and infrastructure providers – the same mix that would make a subsequent webinar and, if useful, a working group productive. The note anchors a live discussion: each section feeds a concrete question a panel can test against real operating examples, without descending into product pitches.

2The core distinction: status versus demonstrable evidence

Most compliance frameworks answer "does the entity have the control?" The harder and more useful question is "can the entity show the control held – continuously – and can someone independent re-derive that later?" These are different problems, and the gap between them is where most operational failures live.

The distinction in one line A point-in-time report is a photograph. Full backing, segregation, redemption at par, effective sanctions screening – these are continuous claims. Assurance that relies only on photographs cannot see the intervals between them, and it is in those intervals that reserves drift, segregation lapses and controls quietly stop firing.

The goal is evidence that survives being challenged – not evidence that is merely produced.

3Three properties that make evidence hold

Three properties separate evidence that withstands scrutiny from evidence that does not. They are technology-agnostic; several methods can deliver them.

Integrity – the evidence cannot be silently altered

The inputs behind a claim (balances, reserve composition, custody statements, reconciliation and screening records) and the resulting conclusion should be bound together so that any later change is detectable.

Reproducibility – a third party can re-derive the same result

A claim should carry enough of a fixed, versioned method that an independent party can take the same inputs and reach the identical conclusion, off the claiming party's own infrastructure.

Continuity – the gaps between checkpoints are addressed

Where feasible, evidence should be event-anchored as well as periodic – sealing a record whenever a material state change occurs – so a continuous claim is supported by continuous evidence.

4Minimum records to make a control demonstrable

For any given control, the note proposes a short, consistent evidence unit:

  • Inputs: the specific data the control acted on (e.g. reserve holdings, wallet balances, the transaction set screened), captured as a canonical, hashable record.
  • Method & version: the fixed rule or procedure applied, with a version identifier.
  • Result: the conclusion reached (pass / exception / value), bound to the inputs and method.
  • Seal: a tamper-evident binding of the above, so undetected later alteration is not possible.
  • Timestamp & owner: when it was produced and the accountable control owner.

5Worked examples

The model is best tested against two very different controls: an NAV calculation and a custody segregation check. Both are familiar to STO Foundation participants, and both show where status reporting stops and demonstrable evidence begins.

Example A: a tokenised fund NAV

To make the model concrete, take a tokenised money-market fund whose NAV must be reconstructed six months later.

The control Daily NAV publication for investors and regulator.
Evidence unitExample value
InputsCustodian statement of T-bill holdings (€9,950,000); 100,000 tokens issued; €50,000 accrued fees.
Method & versionNAV calculation v2.3: (T-bill holdings − accrued fees) / tokens issued.
Result€99.00 per token.
SealA hash of the canonical input set and result, bound to a timestamp.
Timestamp & owner2026-08-10T16:00:00Z; fund accountant, the fund's depositary reconciliation.

Six months later, a regulator can take the same inputs (retrieved from the custodian and on-chain supply) and re-run the same NAV v2.3 formula. If the result is €99.00, the evidence holds. If the inputs do not match, or the result differs without explanation, the problem is visible immediately.

The seal does not prove that the T-bills physically exist or are worth €9.95m; custody and valuation remain the custodian's and auditor's responsibility. The seal proves that the published NAV was derived exactly from those specific inputs, with that specific formula, at that specific time, by that accountable party.

Example B: client-fund segregation at a 30-person French CASP

Now take a newly authorised CASP in France with around thirty staff. On 12 March it must show that client assets were segregated from proprietary assets at the close of business. Six months later, a supervisor asks for the proof.

The control Daily sign-off that client fiat and crypto balances are not commingled with the firm’s own funds.
Evidence unitExample value
InputsCustodian statement for the EUR omnibus account; wallet snapshot of client crypto wallets at 18:00 UTC; internal proprietary balance from the accounting system.
Method & versionSegregation reconciliation v1.7: compare custodian + on-chain client totals to proprietary ledger, flag any overlap.
ResultNo overlap detected; client and proprietary balances are separately identified.
SealHash of the three inputs, the signed reconciliation export and the result, bound to a timestamp.
Timestamp & owner2026-03-12T18:47:00Z; signed by the CFO and countersigned by the MLRO.

Reconstructed six months later, the control stands if the custodian statement, the wallet snapshot, the ledger and the signatures still produce the same hash and the same conclusion. If the reconciliation export is missing, or if the wallet snapshot was taken at 19:00 instead of 18:00, the gap is visible. The seal cannot invent the missing evidence, but it makes the absence explicit.

In practice, this is where most firms currently live. The policy exists, the spreadsheets exist, the email sign-off exists. What is missing is a single, versioned record that binds the inputs, the method, the result and the accountable owner to a single point in time.

What this example does not prove The seal does not prove the custodian actually holds the assets, or that the wallet belongs to the firm and not a third party. Those are custody and legal questions for the auditor and the supervisor. The seal only proves that, on 12 March at 18:47 UTC, the firm ran segregation reconciliation v1.7 on those specific inputs and reached that specific conclusion.

6Mapping representative MiCA & DORA obligations

The model applies to obligations the industry already recognises. Illustrative, not exhaustive:

Obligation (representative)Status viewDemonstrable / re-runnable view

Custody & wallet governancePolicy + sampling-date checksSegregation reconstructable for every day, not only sampling dates
AML/CFT & sanctions screeningPolicy document describing the controlRe-runnable record that the control fired on the relevant transactions at the relevant time
ICT & third-party risk (DORA)Register + point-in-time assessmentTamper-evident evidence that resilience controls operated across the interval
Incident handling (DORA)Post-hoc narrativeSealed, timestamped trail a reviewer can replay after the fact

7The six-month test

A single question operationalises the whole model, and could frame the webinar discussion:

Six months from now, if a regulator, auditor or LP asks about one specific control on one specific past date, can the entity reconstruct – from sealed inputs and a versioned method – that it operated as claimed, off its own say-so?

If the answer is "we would point to the policy" or "we would trust the month-end statement," the control is a status, not demonstrable evidence.

8Unresolved questions for the group

This note deliberately leaves open questions that a webinar panel of issuers, custodians, compliance specialists and infrastructure providers should test against practice:

  • Source of inputs: who is an acceptable source for the custodian statement, NAV price feed or wallet balance? Is a data feed from the operator enough, or should it come from an independent third party?
  • Standardisation vs. flexibility: should the industry agree a single minimum evidence unit, or should each framework keep its own format?
  • Frequency: does every control need event-anchored evidence, or is a periodic snapshot sufficient for low-risk controls?
  • Confidentiality vs. verifiability: how much of the sealed input can be disclosed to an auditor without exposing sensitive positions?
  • Liability boundary: what does a seal prove, and what does it explicitly not prove? Does the market need a standard disclaimer?

10Scope and limitations

This note is deliberate about what the described techniques do and do not establish. Cryptographic sealing and reproducibility prove integrity, reproducibility and authenticity of a stated evidence set and method – that specific inputs produced a specific result, and that neither was altered afterwards. They do not establish the legal truth of the underlying facts, the physical existence or valuation of an asset, or the regulatory sufficiency of a control. Those remain matters for supervisors, qualified auditors and applicable law. In short: these methods prove integrity, not legal truth.
